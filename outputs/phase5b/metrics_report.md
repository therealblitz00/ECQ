## 3. Recommendation

### Aggregation (the "another metric besides the median" for baselines and analogues)

- **Keep the median for the baselines.** Mean of log (geometric mean of units) and quantile 40 are clearly worse (the intervals of the difference exclude 0 on both splits) and bias the estimate down; quantile 60 and the trimmed mean tie with the median on error but shift the bias by +0.18 and -0.07 log. Sales are skewed: the mean of log pulls the typical group down; the median is the robust, simple summary.
- **Analogues, sales:** a trimmed mean (or a median) of the 10 neighbours beats the similarity-weighted mean by -0.024 log units on test (95% interval -0.038 to -0.010), with the same sign on validation. It works by ignoring one or two very weak neighbours, so it over-estimates a little (bias +0.13 log). The gain is about 2% of units, and the delivered sales model does not use the analogues, so it is a refinement of a reference, not a reason to change the model. **Price:** all analogue aggregations are within 0.01 log of each other; the sharpened weights and quantile 60 gain 0.002 to 0.007. The mode (most common price point) is the worst choice for price on both baselines and analogues: the list prices are 40 points, a group has no clear peak and the mode ignores the neighbours' spread.

### Evaluation metric

**Headline: WAPE** (weighted absolute percentage error = sum of the units missed / sum of the real units). Chosen sales model: **38.2%** (baselines 47.6% and 44.4%; intervals in section 2). Price: **11.8%**.

Why: (1) it is the buyer's own question, "how many units is the order away from what sells", in a unit a buyer reads without a log; (2) it weighs each product by its sales, so the best sellers, which carry the turnover, count more than the slow ones (a median error treats a 50-unit and a 5,000-unit product alike); (3) it stays defined for tiny sellers.

Why not the others: **MAPE** is unusable (1399% for the chosen model: a product that sells 3 units and is estimated 80 counts as +2,500%); **MdAPE** (34.6%) is robust but hides the big sellers; **MAE in log** and **RMSLE** (0.856) treat a 2x miss alike on 10 and on 5,000 units and are not readable by a buyer; the **median error** is blind to the tail. The **tercile table** shows the tail: the model under-estimates the best sellers (-29% of their units) and over-estimates the slowest (+51%); read it as the pattern of any estimate that is pulled towards the middle (terciles are cut on the real sales, which exaggerates it), not as a bug.

**Supporting metric 1: over-order versus under-order** (units over and under, each as a share of real units). Chosen sales model: 12.5% too many (stock left) against 25.7% too few (sales lost). The cost of the two is not equal, and only the buyer knows the ratio. The log model leans to under-ordering (net -13.2%); a units re-centring (x1.12, read on validation) balances them (18.2% vs 20.6%, net -2.4%) at a small cost in WAPE (38.8%); not applied here: a decision for the buyer.

**Supporting metric 2: share within 2x** for sales (75.3%; baselines 67.3%) and **share within one price point** for price (77.8%; sub-family median 59.7%). They say how often the estimate is in the right ballpark, which is how an order is checked, and they are not moved by a few extreme products.

The ranking measures of `forecast_report.md` (Spearman within category and in the same launch month) stay as the way to say whether the model orders the products like the market. The 80% ranges are calibrated (section 2: 80% coverage, worst category 78.5%).
